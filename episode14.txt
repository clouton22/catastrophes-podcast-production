This is Catastrophes. Book Nine, Episode Six: The Tanks Stop.

It is October, twenty seventy-two.

Four months have passed since the demonstrations at Peking University became a national movement.

The intervening weeks have not made the situation simpler.

The National Emergency Committee has formed.

Several provincial governments have refused parts of its authority.

Rail workers have staged rolling strikes.

University occupations have spread.

Military units have been moved toward Beijing, then halted, then moved again.

Zhao Wen has been removed from two committees and added to another.

This was apparently intended to reduce her influence.

It did not.

Lin Meiyu was released on October third.

The government hoped this would reduce the demonstrations.

It did.

For about six hours.

Then Lin appeared on the steps of Peking University and said that her release did not answer the question for which she had been arrested.

The crowd became larger.

By October eighth, the National Emergency Committee had concluded that civilian measures were no longer sufficient.

That phrase appears in the order.

“Civilian measures are no longer sufficient.”

It did not say tanks.

It did not say Tiananmen.

It authorized the military to secure transportation corridors, government buildings, communications centers, and designated areas necessary to restore ordinary civil administration.

Those words could describe many things.

By then, everyone knew what they meant.

The orders reached the General Staff shortly after two in the morning on October ninth.

General Liu Jian was awake.

This is not particularly surprising.

Nobody important in Beijing was sleeping much that month.

Liu had spent the previous five weeks preparing for an order he hoped would not arrive.

He had plans.

Routes.

Unit assignments.

Rules of engagement.

Medical support.

Fuel schedules.

He had also spent those five weeks asking increasingly irritating questions about who possessed the legal authority to activate them.

The National Emergency Committee believed it did.

Zhao Wen believed it did not.

The constitutional lawyers had produced approximately enough paper to fortify a small building.

At two-twenty-six, Liu requested written confirmation from the civilian government.

At two-forty-one, it arrived.

At two-forty-seven, he requested confirmation that the President of the State Council had personally approved the order.

He had not.

At three-oh-three, the National Emergency Committee informed Liu that its emergency authority made further confirmation unnecessary.

Liu asked them to put that in writing.

They did.

This would prove unwise.

At three-thirty-eight, the first units were ordered toward central Beijing.

Liu did not refuse the order.

Not yet.

He instructed his commanders to secure their assigned routes and avoid unnecessary confrontation.

No live ammunition was to be chambered without direct authorization.

Armored units were to remain behind infantry formations.

Medical vehicles were to accompany every column.

These instructions were later described as evidence that Liu had already decided to resist.

There is no evidence for that.

He appears to have been trying to preserve as many choices as possible.

That had once been considered a very Progressive virtue.

By twenty seventy-two, it had become a military tactic.

At four-seventeen in the morning, the first armored column entered the eastern approaches to Beijing.

People were already waiting for it.

@chunk 2

Nobody knows who first told the demonstrators the tanks were coming.

There are at least nine people who later claimed responsibility.

Several of them were probably telling the truth.

Telephone calls moved faster than official announcements.

Rail workers passed information from stations.

Military families called relatives.

Students monitored police radio.

Taxi drivers noticed roads closing.

By dawn, crowds were gathering along the eastern avenues.

This was not yet Tiananmen.

That distinction matters.

The first confrontation happened several kilometers away, where a column from the Thirty-Seventh Army encountered several thousand civilians standing across the road.

The soldiers stopped.

Not permanently.

Traffic had stopped in front of them.

The commanding officer ordered the lead vehicles to wait while infantry cleared a passage.

The infantry got out.

Nobody cleared anything.

There are photographs of soldiers standing beside armored vehicles talking to people in pajamas.

One woman brought a thermos.

Someone passed cigarettes through the crowd.

A student climbed onto the front of a personnel carrier and began arguing with a lieutenant about constitutional authority.

The lieutenant, unfortunately, had not brought his constitutional law notes.

For almost forty minutes, nothing happened.

Then the column received an order to proceed.

The lieutenant asked how.

The answer was to use minimum necessary force.

That phrase had appeared in Liu's planning documents weeks earlier.

Now it had become an instruction.

The lieutenant ordered his soldiers to form a line.

The crowd sat down.

This was not part of anyone's plan.

At six-fifty-two, military headquarters received the first request for clarification.

Could soldiers physically move seated civilians?

Yes.

Could they use batons?

Only if necessary.

Could they fire warning shots?

No.

Could vehicles advance if civilians refused to move?

The answer took longer.

At seven-oh-eight, headquarters replied:

“No vehicle is to advance through an occupied civilian position without further authorization.”

That sentence stopped one column.

Then three.

Then seven.

By eight in the morning, armored units were sitting on roads across eastern Beijing.

The tanks had not been ordered to stop.

They had been ordered not to run people over.

The difference would become historically inconvenient.

Inside the Great Hall of the People, the National Emergency Committee demanded that Liu clear the routes.

Liu replied that the routes could be cleared if the government authorized the force required to clear them.

The committee told him he already possessed that authority.

Liu asked whether that included lethal force against unarmed civilians.

Nobody answered directly.

Zhao Wen did.

She had arrived at the meeting shortly after seven despite no longer being a formal member of the committee.

Accounts differ on who let her in.

One claims she simply walked past security.

I prefer this version, but preference is not evidence.

Zhao asked to see the written order sent to Liu.

Someone gave it to her.

She read the paragraph establishing the committee's emergency authority twice.

Then she asked a question.

“Under which constitution?”

The chairman told her that this was not the time for legal argument.

Zhao answered:

“This is exactly when legal arguments matter.”

Outside, more people were sitting down in front of tanks.

Some soldiers were sitting down too.

By nine-thirty, television crews had arrived.

By ten, the images were being transmitted across China.

A tank.

A crowd.

A soldier accepting a cup of tea.

And nothing happening.

For the moment.

@chunk 3

By late morning, the crisis had become public.

That changed everything.

The National Emergency Committee had expected military movement to restore control before the country had time to decide what the movement meant.

Instead, television showed armored columns sitting still.

It showed civilians in the road.

It showed soldiers waiting.

It showed officers making telephone calls.

It showed nothing happening.

For a government trying to demonstrate authority, this was not ideal.

For protesters, it was electrifying.

Crowds grew along the routes into central Beijing.

People brought food.

Water.

Blankets.

Handwritten copies of Zhao Wen's July memorandum.

Someone printed Liu Jian's old annotation from the contingency order.

Whose?

By noon, it was on signs.

Liu did not appreciate this.

He had not intended to contribute a slogan.

At eleven-forty, the National Emergency Committee issued a direct order to clear the eastern avenues by one in the afternoon.

The order authorized nonlethal force.

It did not define what should happen if nonlethal force failed.

Liu requested clarification.

At eleven-fifty-eight, he received it.

Commanders were authorized to take “all measures necessary to restore movement.”

That was closer.

It was still not enough.

Liu asked whether “all measures necessary” included firing on civilians.

The committee replied that commanders should use their professional judgment.

This was the moment.

Not the tanks.

Not the crowds.

Not the television cameras.

A sentence.

Liu had spent his career inside institutions that distributed responsibility carefully.

Now the civilian authority was attempting to return it to him at the exact moment it became dangerous.

He refused to accept it.

At twelve-sixteen, Liu issued an order to all units in the Beijing military district:

“No lethal force will be used against unarmed civilians without an explicit written order identifying the civilian authority authorizing it.”

The order did not tell the tanks to stop.

They were already stopped.

It told the government what it would have to do to make them move.

The National Emergency Committee immediately demanded that Liu rescind it.

He declined.

They threatened to remove him.

Liu replied that they were free to do so.

Then they discovered that replacing the commander of the Beijing military district during an active national crisis required signatures from offices they did not fully control.

This was one of those procedural safeguards everyone had found irritating until approximately twelve-twenty that afternoon.

Zhao Wen later said that the most important thing Liu did was not refuse an order.

It was force the people giving the order to admit what the order actually was.

That distinction became central to the later constitutional settlement.

At the time, it meant that the tanks remained where they were.

At twelve-forty-three, one crew shut off its engine.

Then another.

Then, gradually, across eastern Beijing, armored vehicles began going quiet.

The footage is strange.

Nothing dramatic happens.

Engines stop.

Soldiers climb down.

People remain sitting in the road.

A child walks between two tanks carrying a plastic bag of bread.

For decades afterward, that image would appear beside the phrase The Tanks Stop.

Not because every tank in Beijing stopped at twelve-forty-three.

They did not.

Some continued moving elsewhere.

Some units had never received the order.

Some commanders were still waiting for instructions.

History likes a single moment.

Reality usually requires several.

@chunk 4

The National Emergency Committee tried to solve the problem by changing commanders.

This made the problem larger.

At one-twenty, Liu Jian was formally relieved of operational command.

At one-twenty-seven, his designated replacement, General Peng Rui, declined to assume it until the legality of the committee's order had been reviewed.

At one-thirty-nine, a second officer accepted the appointment.

At one-fifty-two, three regional commanders announced that they would recognize orders from the new command only if those orders were countersigned by the State Council.

At two-oh-four, the State Council refused.

This is the point at which the crisis stopped being primarily about crowds in Beijing.

The state itself had begun disagreeing about who could give an order.

Zhao Wen moved quickly.

She had spent thirty years telling people that continuity mattered.

Now continuity required interruption.

At two-twenty-one, she appeared before a group of senior civil officials and military representatives in the Great Hall.

The meeting was not authorized by the National Emergency Committee.

It was also attended by enough of the government that authorization had become a complicated concept.

Zhao proposed three immediate steps.

Suspend the committee's extraordinary powers.

Return military command to the ordinary constitutional chain.

Convene representatives from the provinces, the Party, the legislature, the courts, and the armed forces to establish an interim political settlement.

Nobody agreed on the third point.

They agreed on the first two much faster.

The National Emergency Committee rejected the proposal.

Several of its members then discovered that their communications staff had stopped forwarding their orders.

A few hours earlier, this would have been called insubordination.

By late afternoon, people were calling it neutrality.

Words change quickly during revolutions.

At three-eleven, Liu Jian appeared on national television.

He was no longer technically in command.

That was why the appearance mattered.

He wore his uniform.

He did not stand beside a flag.

He did not call for the government to fall.

He said:

“The armed forces are responsible to the lawful state and to the people whose state it is. We will not decide the political future of China by force.”

The statement lasted seventy-eight seconds.

The final sentence became famous.

The first seventy seconds are more interesting.

Liu spent most of them telling soldiers to remain at their posts, protect strategic facilities, obey lawful civilian authority, and avoid confrontation.

He was trying to prevent a revolution inside the army while a revolution was happening outside it.

That is a difficult balance.

By four, units across Beijing were reporting that they would not clear civilian gatherings without explicit civilian orders.

No civilian authority was willing to issue one.

The National Emergency Committee still existed.

Its members still occupied offices.

Its documents still carried seals.

Its telephones still worked.

Increasingly, nobody answered them.

This is how governments sometimes end.

Not with a seizure of the palace.

With an instruction that stops producing actions.

By sunset, the crowds around the stalled columns had become almost festive.

This was dangerous.

Nobody yet knew whether another unit would arrive with different orders.

Nobody knew whether hardliners controlled strategic forces outside Beijing.

Nobody knew whether the government would fragment peacefully or violently overnight.

The tanks had stopped.

The crisis had not.

@chunk 5

The night of October ninth was less dramatic than people remember.

That is not the same as saying it was calm.

There were rumors of arrests.

Rumors of counterorders.

Rumors that strategic missile units had changed command codes.

Rumors that Zhao Wen had been detained.

She had not.

Rumors that Liu Jian had fled Beijing.

He had not.

Rumors that the National Emergency Committee had dissolved.

It had not.

What had happened was harder to describe.

Authority had become uneven.

Some ministries continued obeying the committee.

Others reverted to the State Council.

Provincial governments began choosing which orders they regarded as valid.

Military commanders requested confirmation from civilian offices that were themselves arguing about who possessed authority to confirm anything.

For several hours, China had a government in the grammatical sense more clearly than in the practical one.

The most dangerous question involved the strategic forces.

The World War had left China with a political culture deeply suspicious of ever allowing its security to depend upon another country's restraint.

That meant atomic weapons.

Missiles.

Submarines.

Command aircraft.

And procedures designed to ensure that a sudden attack could not eliminate the government's ability to respond.

Those procedures assumed the government itself still knew who was giving orders.

At six-forty that evening, Liu Jian requested a temporary freeze on changes to strategic-force readiness.

Not disarmament.

Not de-alerting.

No new authentication codes.

No movement to higher readiness.

No transfer of launch authority.

Existing positions would hold until a recognized civilian chain of command could be established.

The National Emergency Committee ordered the strategic command to reject Liu's request.

The State Council ordered it to accept.

The commander of the strategic forces, General Sun Wei, chose a third option.

She asked both sides to identify the constitutional authority under which they were acting.

This did not speed things up.

At seven-twenty-three, Sun issued what later became known as the Hold Order.

All strategic units were to maintain existing posture.

No launch authority would be transferred.

No readiness increase would occur without authentication from both the State Council and the standing military command structure.

The order was legally improvised.

Probably.

The arguments about that are excellent and very long.

Its immediate effect was simpler.

Nobody could use the confusion in Beijing to seize control of the atomic arsenal.

Zhao supported the order.

Liu supported it.

Several hardliners called it military obstruction.

They were not entirely wrong.

They were also unable to make anyone reverse it.

By midnight, provincial leaders were openly demanding a national conference.

The Party leadership had split.

The National Emergency Committee had lost effective control of most military commands.

And crowds still filled Tiananmen.

There had been deaths by then.

A clash near a communications building killed six protesters and two police officers.

Another confrontation outside a provincial government office left eleven dead.

Dozens more had been seriously injured across the country.

Seventy-Two was not bloodless.

It is sometimes described that way because people know what did not happen.

That is a dangerous way to count.

The important fact is not that nobody died.

It is that the machinery capable of killing hundreds of thousands did not start.

By October tenth, Zhao Wen was no longer arguing about how to preserve the existing government.

She was arguing about what should replace the authority that had failed.

@chunk 6

The negotiations began in Beijing.

They did not stay there.

Provincial leaders wanted distance from the ministries they no longer trusted.

Military representatives wanted a location where no single command controlled the streets.

Student groups wanted somewhere that did not look like surrendering the movement back to the capital.

Several cities were proposed.

Nanjing won mostly because everybody disliked it for different reasons.

This is an underrated quality in compromise locations.

The first constitutional conference opened there on November twenty-second, twenty seventy-two.

It did not produce the Nanjing Settlement.

Not immediately.

It produced arguments.

About elections.

About the Party.

About military command.

About emergency powers.

About provincial authority.

About whether Zhao Wen was a reformer, a traitor, or merely the person who had arrived at the correct position inconveniently late.

Zhao herself preferred the last description.

Liu Jian attended only the military sessions.

He resisted every suggestion that the army should serve as guarantor of the political transition.

This frustrated people who wanted a guarantor.

His answer was consistent.

If the settlement depended upon the military deciding whether civilian government remained acceptable, then they had not solved the problem.

By January, the Party had agreed to surrender its constitutional monopoly.

By February, independent political organizations were operating openly in several provinces.

The armed forces were formally transferred from Party command to the state.

Emergency authorities created during the World War were suspended pending legislative reauthorization.

Competitive national elections would follow.

The final Nanjing Settlement was signed in March of twenty seventy-three.

That is the conventional date.

There are, naturally, arguments.

We will spare ourselves one of them.

Zhao Wen emerged from Nanjing as the most important civilian reform figure in the country.

Liu Jian returned to military service long enough to oversee the transfer of command authority and then retired.

Neither had begun the year intending to dismantle the political system they served.

That matters.

Seventy-Two is sometimes told as a story in which reformers defeated hardliners.

That happened.

It is also a story about institutions discovering that obedience could no longer answer the question of legitimacy.

And about people inside those institutions deciding, at different moments and for different reasons, not to force an answer.

I had spent months thinking of Tiananmen as the place I had left.

When I returned the following spring, the tanks and banners were gone.

It looked smaller than I remembered.

It had already become a place everyone remembered differently.

I did not stay at Peking University.

That autumn, I transferred to the University of Chicago.

The next episode is about what happened after the revolution acquired a constitution.

And about the uncomfortable discovery that winning the right to change a system does not tell you what to build in its place.

Thanks for listening.
