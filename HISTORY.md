# Catastrophes — Canonical History

This document records the canonical historical sequence for the **Catastrophes** alternate-history project.

It describes history as it exists within the fictional world. It should not use comparisons to an alternate or external history as part of the in-universe material.

Dates and details marked as approximate or unresolved may be refined later. Established details should not be silently changed without updating this document.

---

# I. Before the Divergence

## The Progressive Era

The early twentieth-century United States experiences a Progressive reform movement concerned with:

- industrial safety
- monopoly power
- political corruption
- railroad regulation
- public health
- labor conditions
- concentrated economic power

The movement is politically significant but fragmented.

Robert M. La Follette Sr. becomes one of its most important national figures.

By the early 1920s, Progressivism appears to many Americans to be a political tradition whose strongest period has already passed.

That changes after 1925.

---

# II. Titanic

## 1912 — The near miss

RMS Titanic departs Southampton on her maiden voyage in April 1912.

She receives multiple reports of ice in the North Atlantic.

On the night of April 14, lookouts Frederick Fleet and Reginald Lee see an iceberg ahead.

First Officer William Murdoch orders evasive action.

Titanic narrowly avoids the iceberg.

The exact distance is disputed in later testimony.

No significant flooding occurs.

Nobody dies.

Titanic arrives in New York on April 17.

---

## The investigation

The near miss receives attention from:

- White Star Line
- the British Board of Trade
- insurers
- engineers
- maritime-safety officials
- newspapers

Issues discussed include:

- lifeboat capacity
- ice warnings
- wireless procedures
- lookout practices
- emergency drills
- increasing ship size
- assumptions surrounding watertight construction

Titanic carries more lifeboats than British law requires but not enough for everyone aboard.

Several recommendations are discussed.

Most are not immediately adopted.

The central problem is interpretation.

One possible lesson is:

> Titanic was lucky.

The more common lesson is:

> The system worked.

Lookouts saw the iceberg.

Officers reacted.

The ship turned.

Everyone survived.

The near miss therefore fails to produce the regulatory transformation it might otherwise have caused.

---

## Titanic's later life

Titanic later serves during the Great War.

She survives wartime service and returns to civilian passenger operations.

By the 1930s, she is increasingly expensive and obsolete.

Titanic is withdrawn from service and scrapped in 1936.

Her long, largely uneventful working life becomes important to later historical interpretations.

---

# III. The Columbia Disaster

## May 20, 1925

The American passenger liner **Columbia** is one of the most prestigious ships operated by the Atlantic-American Line.

She carries:

- 2,847 passengers and crew
- modern pumps
- watertight compartments
- wireless communications
- emergency generators
- legally compliant lifesaving equipment

She does not carry enough lifeboat capacity for everyone aboard.

This is legal.

---

## Samuel Thomas Ward

Samuel Thomas Ward is approximately twenty-three years old.

He is a railway employee from Chicago working for the Great Lakes and Western Railway.

He has spent several months in Britain assisting with negotiations over imported signaling equipment.

His younger sister Margaret is two years old.

Samuel writes home shortly before departing Britain.

He complains about English coffee, looks forward to baseball, and asks whether Margaret has stopped throwing food at dinner.

He boards Columbia for the return trip.

---

## Ice warnings

Columbia receives multiple ice reports.

Captain Edward Harlan responds.

The ship:

- changes course south
- reduces speed
- doubles lookouts
- continues collecting wireless reports

These actions are broadly considered prudent by contemporary standards.

At approximately 4:19 a.m., a lookout sees ice directly ahead.

At approximately 4:23 a.m., Columbia strikes it.

---

## The sinking

The collision is not initially perceived as catastrophic.

Multiple forward compartments flood.

The watertight system functions largely as designed, but flooding exceeds the assumptions under which the system was designed.

Columbia's first wireless message says:

> STRUCK ICE. TAKING WATER. STANDING BY.

The crew does not initially believe the ship will sink.

A full distress call is not transmitted until approximately thirty-nine minutes after the collision.

The delay later becomes controversial.

It is not primarily the result of recklessness.

The crew believes it is still determining whether a serious but manageable emergency has become an evacuation crisis.

---

## Lifeboats

Columbia does not have enough lifeboats for everyone aboard.

This complies with existing regulations.

The regulations are based partly on older assumptions that:

- modern liners are themselves primary survival vessels
- watertight compartments will limit flooding
- wireless will summon nearby ships
- lifeboats can transfer passengers rather than hold everyone simultaneously

These assumptions fail together.

---

## Casualties

The British liner Baltic Star reaches the area shortly after Columbia sinks.

Final count:

- **744 survivors**
- **2,103 dead or missing**

Samuel Ward is initially listed among the rescued.

His parents see the report.

His name is later removed.

His body is never recovered.

Margaret Ward is two years old and later says she has no memory of the disaster.

Thomas Ward remembers it for the rest of his life.

---

# IV. Columbia and the Progressive Revival

## Investigations

The federal government conducts extensive investigations.

The central political question gradually changes.

Initially:

> Who caused Columbia to sink?

Later:

> Why did a system containing many people who knew about individual problems fail to produce action?

Investigators discover that many weaknesses exposed by Columbia had previously been discussed.

---

## Helen Mercer

Commerce Department researcher **Helen Mercer** reviews historical correspondence concerning North Atlantic ice reporting.

She discovers references to the 1912 Titanic near miss.

The underlying records reveal that many categories of risk later exposed by Columbia had already appeared together in discussions after Titanic.

The discovery becomes politically explosive.

---

## The Columbia Families

Families of victims begin organizing.

They seek:

- bodies
- explanations
- records
- regulatory change

They become skilled at obtaining government documents and providing them to reporters.

---

## Thomas Ward

Thomas Ward is Samuel and Margaret's father.

He is an accountant, born approximately between 1877 and 1882.

He travels to Washington initially because Samuel had been falsely listed as rescued.

He becomes increasingly involved with other Columbia families.

By the following year, he is secretary of the Illinois Columbia Families Committee.

Thomas's political importance comes partly from his instinct for:

- records
- documentation
- accounting
- discrepancies
- institutional responsibility

He remains associated with the idea that failures often become visible when someone starts keeping track of the right things.

Thomas plausibly remains alive into the late 1950s or early 1960s.

---

## Robert La Follette Jr.

Robert M. La Follette Sr. dies shortly before the Columbia disaster.

His son, **Robert La Follette Jr.**, becomes one of the politicians most interested in the systemic implications of Columbia.

A central question emerges:

> What if nobody broke the rule, but the rule was the problem?

This helps revive Progressive political thought.

---

# V. Competitive Catastrophe

By the late 1920s, Progressive writers increasingly describe situations in which:

- each participant behaves rationally
- each participant pursues reasonable local interests
- nobody intends catastrophe
- the combined outcome becomes dangerous

The phrase **competitive catastrophe** becomes associated with this concept.

Shipping provides the first major example.

A shipping company may prefer additional safety equipment but suffer competitively if rivals are not required to adopt it.

Regulation can therefore allow all competitors to take an action none can rationally take alone.

The concept soon expands beyond shipping.

---

# VI. The Depression and Progressive Realignment

## 1929

The stock market crashes.

The crash is not treated as the sole cause of the Depression.

The broader crisis includes:

- banking weakness
- debt
- declining demand
- agricultural distress
- monetary problems
- international financial instability

Politically, however, Americans have recently learned a particular language for systemic failure.

Depositors withdraw money because banks look weak.

Banks call loans because they need cash.

Businesses cut production because demand falls.

Employers lay off workers because sales fall.

Workers spend less because they have lost jobs.

Every action can be individually rational.

Together they deepen the crisis.

---

## Franklin Roosevelt

Franklin Delano Roosevelt becomes a major national leader.

He does not invent the revived Progressive movement.

Instead, his particular political skill is recognizing and holding together a coalition that already exists.

The Democratic Party is increasingly divided among:

- Southern conservatives
- urban machines
- labor
- northern reformers
- western populists
- business Democrats

The Progressive Party remains an independent political force.

---

## 1932 and the Madison Agreement

The presidential contest includes:

- Herbert Hoover
- Franklin Roosevelt
- a Progressive candidacy associated with the La Follette movement

Roosevelt negotiates cooperation with Progressive leaders.

The resulting arrangement is commonly associated with the **Madison Agreement**.

Progressives support Roosevelt where splitting the reform vote could preserve Republican control.

Roosevelt agrees to support significant Progressive economic and regulatory reforms.

The Progressive Party remains organizationally independent.

Roosevelt wins.

---

## The Progressive state

The Roosevelt administration develops policies including:

- stronger banking regulation
- deposit insurance
- securities regulation
- unemployment relief
- public works
- industrial safety regulation
- systematic collection of near-miss data

A major conceptual change occurs:

An event does not have to kill someone before it can count as evidence.

The political principle develops that:

> A regulation written in blood was written too late.

---

# VII. Preparedness

Progressive systems thinking begins influencing military and strategic policy.

The central tension is between two historical lessons.

The Great War suggests:

> Mobilization, fear, alliance commitments, and escalating responses can create the war governments claim to be preventing.

Columbia suggests:

> Waiting until danger becomes catastrophe is too late.

Roosevelt increasingly supports preparedness without immediate intervention.

This includes:

- factories
- aircraft production
- ports
- rail capacity
- naval readiness
- strategic materials
- training
- electrical infrastructure

Redundant or unused capacity increasingly becomes understood as potential resilience rather than simple waste.

---

# VIII. The Fascist War

## Naming

The conflict initially receives names including **Second World War**.

During 1940, American public usage increasingly shifts toward:

**the Fascist War**

The name identifies an ideological enemy and a cause rather than simply describing a second global conflict.

The government eventually adopts the terminology after public usage has already made it common.

---

## Battle of Hawaii

Japan intends a surprise strike against the American Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor.

American preparedness has changed the circumstances.

The Pacific Fleet is already at sea conducting active exercises.

The intended raid becomes a surprise fleet battle.

The United States suffers significant losses but wins strategically.

Japan loses experienced carrier pilots and fails to achieve the expected period of uncontested operational freedom.

The American cultural memory becomes:

> They tried to catch us asleep. We were ready.

---

## European war

The Western Allies land in France earlier than in familiar expectations of the conflict.

Germany faces:

- Allied material superiority
- Soviet pressure from the east
- internal political fracture

In 1944, senior German military and civilian figures move against Nazi leadership.

The German state fragments.

Organized German resistance ends by August 1944.

---

# IX. The Atomic Demonstration

## Manhattan Project

The Manhattan Project begins largely from fear that Germany may develop atomic weapons first.

After Germany collapses, the project continues.

The first successful American atomic test occurs in July 1944.

Japan remains at war.

---

## The decision

American officials debate using the weapon against a populated target.

Arguments against immediate city use include:

- moral objections
- uncertainty over whether one destroyed city would force surrender
- concern about establishing the precedent for future atomic warfare
- desire to demonstrate the weapon internationally
- concern about irreversible decisions

President Roosevelt chooses a demonstration.

The reasoning is not that a demonstration is guaranteed to work.

It is that failure leaves another decision available.

Destroying a city does not.

---

## September 2, 1944

An atomic device is detonated over an uninhabited artificial target complex in the American Southwest.

International observers attend.

The target includes:

- structures
- rail equipment
- vehicles
- bridgework
- aircraft components
- instrumentation

The public's first image of an atomic explosion is destruction without bodies.

The weapon is terrifying partly because the images show absence.

Structures existed.

Then they did not.

---

## Japanese surrender

Japan does not surrender immediately.

Relevant pressures include:

- naval collapse
- blockade
- conventional bombing
- the atomic demonstration
- the threat of Soviet entry into the Pacific war
- internal Japanese political conflict
- the status of the Emperor

The United States clarifies that Japan must surrender its armed forces and accept occupation, but the Emperor may remain subject to occupation authority.

This creates a settlement that different participants can describe differently.

The Japanese government can say it preserved the throne.

The United States can say it did not negotiate away the surrender.

This becomes an early example of a diplomatic technique later refined and used again.

Japan surrenders in December 1944.

The Fascist War ends.

No atomic weapon has been used against a human target.

---

# X. The Atomic Problem

The United States possesses atomic weapons.

The Soviet Union does not.

American scientists and officials understand that the monopoly cannot remain permanent.

A small group of:

- lawyers
- diplomats
- engineers
- scientists
- military officers

begins considering international control.

This group becomes a recurring thread through the early postwar episodes.

Margaret Ward gradually becomes part of it.

---

# XI. Stalin and the Soviet Crisis

Early international-control proposals fail.

Stalin rejects meaningful outside inspection and pursues a Soviet atomic program.

The Soviet state enters a major political and military crisis earlier than it otherwise might have been expected to.

Stalin is removed or falls during this crisis.

Two important surviving figures are:

- **Georgy Zhukov**
- **Alexei Morozov**

Morozov is a fictional Soviet reformer.

His central institutional principle is:

> A socialist system unable to report its own failures cannot correct them.

This becomes an important Soviet contribution to later Progressive systems thinking.

---

# XII. The Soviet Bomb

The Soviet Union successfully demonstrates an atomic device.

The American monopoly is definitively over.

Both governments now confront the same basic problem:

Neither trusts the other with an independent atomic arsenal.

Neither can maintain a permanent monopoly.

Other industrial states can eventually build weapons as well.

---

# XIII. The Atomic Settlement

## The starting principle

The breakthrough does not come from increased trust.

Distrust becomes a design assumption.

The order becomes:

> Inspection. Control. Verification.

Trust, if it appears at all, can come later.

The system assumes someone will eventually attempt to cheat.

---

## Margaret Ward

Margaret Ward is increasingly important in the legal design of the system.

Her role should not be exaggerated into sole authorship.

Scientists understand materials.

Engineers understand facilities.

Diplomats understand what governments may accept.

Military officers understand concealment.

Margaret and other lawyers define what inspection authority actually means.

A principle associated with her is:

> An inspection right that required permission at the moment of inspection was not an inspection right. It was a request.

---

## Accounting

The proposed system focuses on controlling weapons-usable material rather than merely counting finished bombs.

Relevant activities include:

- uranium mining
- enrichment
- plutonium production
- chemical separation
- reactor operation
- fissile-material inventories

Facilities capable of producing weapons material operate under international accounting rules.

According to Ward family papers, **Thomas Ward, the accountant**, considers this the first sensible sentence anyone has written about atomic weapons.

---

## The proliferation breakthrough

David Lilienthal and Igor Kurchatov participate in a critical Geneva technical session.

Kurchatov is associated with the observation:

> Two is not the dangerous number.

When Lilienthal asks what number is dangerous, Kurchatov replies approximately:

> Any number that teaches the next country it must become one more.

Exact wording is disputed.

The insight is clear.

An American arsenal justifies a Soviet arsenal.

Two arsenals justify a British program.

Three make a French program easier to defend.

Every new program becomes evidence for the next.

The acquisition process reproduces itself.

---

## Internationalization

The American and Soviet delegations agree that other nations must be invited.

Otherwise the system will not work.

This complicates the negotiations.

It probably saves them.

Countries without atomic weapons demand a role in governing the states that possess them.

Participants include countries such as:

- India
- Sweden
- Canada
- Brazil
- Czechoslovakia

The bomb increasingly becomes treated not as an American or Soviet possession but as a category of systemic hazard.

---

## International Atomic Authority

The **International Atomic Authority**, or IAA, is established in Geneva in 1953.

Its major responsibilities include:

1. controlling production of weapons-usable material;
2. inspecting declared facilities and investigating suspicious facilities;
3. certifying dismantlement of national atomic arsenals.

The Authority develops an international profession of:

- physicists
- chemists
- engineers
- accountants
- lawyers
- former military officers

The culture emphasizes:

- suspicion without accusation
- documentation
- material accounting
- verification
- investigation of discrepancies

---

## Dismantlement

Neither superpower agrees to dismantle first.

Dismantlement therefore proceeds reciprocally.

American and Soviet weapons are disassembled in defined groups.

International observers verify that weapons cease functioning without necessarily receiving unrestricted access to every engineering secret.

Fissile material moves into monitored storage or civilian use.

At a later inspection conference, empty American and Soviet weapon cases are photographed together.

A reporter asks Morozov whether the image represents trust.

Morozov replies:

> No.

It represents accounting.

---

## Hydrogen bomb problem

Thermonuclear research raises the possibility of weapons much more powerful than early atomic devices.

The IAA expands its authority to make secret advanced weapons programs:

- expensive
- slow
- detectable
- politically dangerous

The objective is not perfect prevention.

It is:

- warning
- time
- evidence
- another opportunity to act before an outcome becomes irreversible

---

## Zero national arsenals

By 1957:

- the United States no longer maintains an operational national atomic arsenal;
- the Soviet Union no longer maintains one;
- Britain has abandoned its independent weapons effort before producing a deployable force.

Other states gain access to civilian nuclear technology and internationally supplied fuel without developing independent weapons programs.

The IAA becomes part:

- regulator
- inspection service
- technical network
- fuel coordinator
- diplomatic institution

A generation grows up viewing national atomic arsenals as a dangerous experiment that governments briefly attempted and then abandoned.
