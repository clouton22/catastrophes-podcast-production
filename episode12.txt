@chunk 1

This is Catastrophes. Book Eight, Episode Nine: The Second Shanghai Crisis.

It is now twenty sixty-five.

The World War has been over for twenty-three years.

Depending on which date you use.

We have already had that argument.

By twenty sixty-five, atomic weapons were no longer new.

That may be the most important thing to understand about the Second Shanghai Crisis.

The people making decisions that week had grown up with them.

The shelters were old.

The warning systems had been upgraded twice.

Missile submarines had crews whose parents had served on missile submarines.

Strategic doctrine had acronyms, graduate programs, professional journals, and arguments sufficiently specialized that intelligent people could spend entire careers disagreeing about the meaning of one sentence in a targeting policy.

This was not the frightened improvisation of twenty forty.

It was a system.

The United States and China possessed the largest arsenals.

Russia retained a smaller one.

India had one.

Several European states participated in shared arrangements that everyone insisted were not quite the same thing.

There were treaties limiting numbers.

There were inspection regimes.

There were hotlines.

There were launch notifications.

There were procedures for distinguishing an exercise from an attack.

There were procedures for what to do when those procedures failed.

Most of the time, they worked.

That was the problem with studying the Second Shanghai Crisis afterward.

Almost every individual part of the system performed approximately as designed.

On October fourteenth, twenty sixty-five, China launched a rocket from Hainan.

The launch had been announced.

The notification had been filed through the usual channels.

The payload was a weather satellite.

This was all true.

At almost the same time, the People's Liberation Army Navy began a previously scheduled exercise in the northern South China Sea.

Chinese strategic missile forces also entered a routine communications test.

An American early-warning satellite failed.

These events were unrelated.

For approximately six hours, almost nobody knew that.

The failed satellite removed one of the systems Washington used to confirm the trajectory of Chinese launches.

The naval exercise increased encrypted military traffic.

The missile communications test produced a pattern American analysts associated with elevated readiness.

And the Hainan rocket appeared in the surviving warning network without the independent confirmation that normally made an announced launch boring.

No single observation indicated attack.

Together, they looked like preparation.

The United States raised the readiness of several Pacific commands.

Not to attack China.

To make sure China could not exploit a temporary gap in American warning coverage.

Chinese surveillance systems detected the readiness change.

Beijing raised its own readiness.

Not to attack the United States.

To make sure the United States could not exploit a moment of Chinese vulnerability.

By late afternoon, both governments were taking defensive measures against defensive measures taken in response to defensive measures.

Nobody had lied.

Nobody had panicked.

Nobody had yet made an obvious mistake.

This is where the story becomes unpleasant.

@chunk 2

The first American assessment reached the White House shortly after four in the afternoon.

It was cautious.

That mattered.

The analysts did not say China was preparing an attack.

They said the pattern was consistent with preparations that would be expected before one.

Those are not the same sentence.

They can produce similar decisions.

The President authorized a limited readiness increase.

Pacific air-defense units were dispersed.

Several submarines changed patrol areas.

Long-range aircraft were moved away from bases considered vulnerable to a first strike.

A small number of strategic bombers were placed on alert.

None of these actions crossed the formal threshold for general mobilization.

That was intentional.

The entire point was to increase survivability without signaling offensive intent.

Chinese intelligence saw the dispersals.

It did not see the sentence explaining them.

Beijing's first assumption was that the United States was protecting its forces because it expected those forces to be needed.

Chinese missile units were ordered to increase mobility.

Command aircraft were prepared.

Senior officials moved into hardened facilities.

The American warning system saw those movements too.

There is a temptation, when describing crises like this, to identify the first mistake.

Historians have tried.

Was it the failed satellite?

The Chinese communications test?

The American dispersal order?

The decision to move Chinese missile units?

The answer is unsatisfying.

Most of those decisions were reasonable.

That is why the crisis is important.

By six-thirty, the United States had evidence that Chinese strategic forces were moving.

China had evidence that American strategic forces were dispersing.

Both governments knew the other side could see at least some of what they were doing.

That made stopping harder.

If Washington reversed its alert while Chinese missiles were still moving, it risked creating vulnerability.

If Beijing stopped moving missiles while American aircraft remained dispersed, it risked doing the same.

The problem was no longer simply what either side believed.

It was what each side believed the other side would infer from changing course.

At seven-twelve, an American Pacific command requested permission to move additional aircraft out of Shanghai-range bases.

Permission was granted.

At seven-forty, Chinese coastal air defenses shifted to wartime tracking procedures.

At eight-oh-three, an American submarine commander received orders to move farther from the Chinese coast.

At eight-seventeen, Chinese naval forces interpreted that movement as possible preparation for a missile-launch corridor.

None of these actions was an attack.

Every one of them made an attack easier to imagine.

The old Bush review procedures were still part of the American system.

By twenty sixty-five, almost nobody called them that formally.

But the requirement remained: when several agencies appeared to be describing the same crisis, someone had to ask whether they were actually describing one event or several unrelated ones.

That question was asked.

It was not answered quickly enough.

@chunk 3

Colonel Chen Lihua was not supposed to be the person who stopped the crisis.

That is usually how these stories work.

Chen was an aerospace liaison officer assigned to the Strategic Assessment Bureau in Beijing.

Her job was mostly procedural.

Launch notifications.

Telemetry review.

Coordination between civilian space agencies and military warning commands.

The sort of work that becomes very important only after something has gone wrong.

At eight-forty-six that evening, Chen was reviewing the American alert changes when she asked to see the original Hainan launch packet.

Not the intelligence summary.

Not the readiness assessment.

The launch packet.

There was some resistance.

The packet had already been reviewed.

The launch had already been incorporated into the strategic picture.

Chen asked again.

The document was eventually brought up on a secondary terminal.

She recognized the mission number.

She had signed part of the clearance.

The Hainan rocket was carrying a weather satellite.

Not a reconnaissance platform.

Not a decoy.

Not an orbital weapons package.

A weather satellite.

Chen later described the moment as less dramatic than people wanted it to be.

She did not stand up.

She did not shout.

She checked the number twice.

Then she asked why the launch notification had not been matched to the readiness assessment.

The answer took several minutes.

The civilian launch registry and the strategic warning network had been operating on separate emergency data systems since the satellite failure.

The notification existed.

The warning existed.

They had not met.

Chen then asked about the naval exercise.

Scheduled.

The missile communications test.

Scheduled.

The American satellite failure.

Confirmed accidental.

One by one, the pieces began to separate.

This did not make the crisis disappear.

American aircraft were still dispersed.

Chinese missile units were still moving.

Submarines were still changing patrol areas.

Commanders still had orders written for a situation that had not existed when those orders were issued.

But the original pattern had changed.

Chen later wrote one sentence that appears in almost every history of the crisis:

“So far, everybody had been right.”

She did not mean correct.

She meant rational.

The Chinese launch was real.

The American warning gap was real.

The readiness changes were real.

The Chinese response to those changes was real.

No fabricated intelligence had been introduced.

No rogue commander had invented an attack.

No computer had hallucinated one.

The danger came from accurate information being assembled into the wrong story.

That was the part people found difficult to accept afterward.

Systems are easier to repair when something breaks.

Shanghai was a system in which almost nothing had.

@chunk 4

Chen's report reached the Chinese crisis staff shortly after nine-thirty.

By then, the problem was no longer proving that the original pattern had been misleading.

The problem was getting two governments to believe that fact at the same time.

China could not simply return its missile forces to normal readiness.

Not while American aircraft remained dispersed.

The United States could not simply recall those aircraft.

Not while Chinese missile units were still moving.

Each side now possessed evidence that the other was escalating.

The fact that both had begun for defensive reasons did not make the evidence disappear.

This was where the old crisis machinery mattered.

The emergency hotline between Beijing and Washington had been established after the World War and revised repeatedly over the next two decades.

It had redundant circuits.

Independent authentication.

Separate civilian and military channels.

And, after a series of reforms in the twenty fifties, a requirement that each government transmit not merely what it intended to do next, but what observable action the other side should expect to see if that statement were true.

This sounds bureaucratic.

It was.

It was also useful.

At ten-oh-seven, Beijing informed Washington that selected missile units would stop moving.

Not return to their bases.

Stop moving.

American surveillance would be able to confirm that.

Washington replied that no additional strategic aircraft would disperse from Pacific bases for the next ninety minutes.

China could observe that too.

Neither side was being asked for trust.

They were being asked to make a small claim that the other could verify.

Then another.

Then another.

It took hours.

A Chinese missile brigade halted beside a highway outside Nanjing.

Three American bombers remained on the ground at Guam.

A command aircraft returned to Beijing.

Two American submarines received orders to hold position.

Chinese air defenses reduced their tracking status one region at a time.

The crisis did not end with a speech.

It became smaller.

At eleven-fifty-two, American analysts confirmed that the first Chinese missile units had stopped moving.

At twelve-nineteen, Beijing confirmed that no additional American bomber dispersals had occurred.

At one-fourteen in the morning, the United States lowered the readiness of several Pacific commands.

China followed thirty-eight minutes later.

By dawn, neither government believed an attack was imminent.

That is usually given as the end of the Second Shanghai Crisis.

There are people who prefer six-forty the following morning, when the first American aircraft returned to its normal base.

Others prefer nine-twelve, when China's strategic command formally cancelled its emergency order.

I have less sympathy for this argument than I do for the one about when the World War ended.

The important point is that, sometime before breakfast, two governments stopped preparing for a war neither had intended to begin.

And then both of them tried very hard to make sure nobody learned how close they had come.

@chunk 5

The public explanation was technical.

An early-warning satellite had failed.

Several military exercises had overlapped.

Readiness procedures had produced unnecessary concern.

The systems had worked.

There would be a review.

Most of that was true.

Almost none of it conveyed what had happened.

The classified investigations were less comforting.

The American review found that every major readiness decision had been supported by information available at the time.

The Chinese review reached essentially the same conclusion.

No one had fabricated evidence.

No commander had exceeded authority.

No warning system had independently produced a false attack.

The crisis had emerged from the interaction between correct procedures.

This was not an unfamiliar idea.

It had simply been a long time since anyone had needed it this badly.

One of the congressional hearings included a first-term senator from New York named Anna Roosevelt.

The name attracted more attention than the question she asked.

“If every office followed procedure, who was responsible for noticing that the procedures were interacting?”

The witness did not have an answer.

One of the investigators on the American side requested a Bush review.

George H. W. Bush had been dead for years by then, but his name still survived in the procedure.

As a reminder, the idea was simple: take the reports apart, put the original observations back on the table, and separate what the evidence actually said from what institutions had inferred from one another.

The resulting diagram became famous later.

A rocket launch.

A satellite failure.

A naval exercise.

An American readiness increase.

A Chinese readiness increase.

Another American readiness increase.

Another Chinese response.

Arrows connected each box.

None of the arrows pointed to an original decision to attack.

That was difficult to explain politically.

It was also the most important finding.

The investigations remained secret.

The diagram did not.

A copy reached the economist Lin Meiyu several years later.

Lin had been born in Shanghai in twenty thirty-two, the year before the World War began. Her family left the city during the early fighting and returned after the peace.

She studied economics at Fudan and later at Peking University, specializing first in reconstruction finance and then in the economics of national security.

By the twenty sixties, Lin had spent much of her career studying defense expenditure, trade disruption, and the strange economic behavior of states that believed they were becoming more secure.

She looked at the Shanghai diagram and recognized something older.

The problem was not irrationality.

The problem was that rational behavior inside one part of the system could make rational behavior inside another part more dangerous.

Her book, published in twenty seventy under the title The Security Trap, did not begin with Shanghai.

It began with a bank run.

That choice irritated several reviewers.

Lin's point was that the mechanism was the same.

A depositor who believed a bank might fail was rational to withdraw money.

Enough rational withdrawals made the bank fail.

A government that believed an adversary might strike was rational to make its forces harder to destroy.

Enough rational preparations made the adversary believe a strike was coming.

Lin wrote:

“Security pursued independently becomes insecurity produced collectively.”

The sentence became famous.

The government disliked the book considerably more than it disliked the sentence.

Because Lin's argument did not stop with nuclear doctrine.

If the security trap was real, then the question was not simply whether Chinese leaders had made good decisions during Shanghai.

It was whether the political system allowed anyone to discover when individually good decisions were combining into something catastrophic.

That was a much less comfortable question.

By twenty seventy-one, university seminars were assigning the book.

By twenty seventy-two, students were carrying quotations from it into the streets.

We will get there.

First, we need to talk about what the Chinese government did when people began asking why nobody had been told how close Shanghai came.

@chunk 6

The first public references to the Shanghai records appeared in twenty seventy-one.

Not the full files.

A memorandum.

Then part of the American review.

Then a Chinese readiness log whose authenticity the government initially refused to confirm.

The leaks were incomplete enough that almost every faction could use them.

Hardliners argued that Shanghai proved how quickly the United States could move toward war.

Reformers argued that it proved how dangerous secrecy had become.

Military officials complained that civilians did not understand readiness procedures.

Civilian officials replied that this was precisely why the procedures required explanation.

Lin Meiyu became increasingly difficult for the government to ignore.

The Security Trap had already been popular in universities.

Now journalists were asking her to explain Shanghai.

She kept giving the same answer.

There had been no single villain.

This was not reassuring.

It was much easier to punish a bad commander than to admit that competent institutions had nearly produced a catastrophe together.

By early twenty seventy-two, copies of the Shanghai diagram were circulating on university campuses.

Students drew it on classroom boards.

Newspapers printed simplified versions.

Someone at Peking University replaced the arrows with a single phrase:

SECURITY WITHOUT FEAR.

Nobody seems to know who did that first.

By March, the phrase was appearing on posters.

By April, it was on walls.

The government ordered several publications to stop reproducing classified material.

Editors complied.

Students copied it by hand.

Lin was detained for questioning in May.

She was released two days later.

Then detained again in June.

The second time, she did not come home.

That was when the demonstrations stopped being mostly about Shanghai.

This is important.

Revolutions are often remembered by the issue that finally fills the square.

They usually begin with something smaller.

A document.

An arrest.

A question the government has answered too many times without actually answering.

The Shanghai Crisis had occurred seven years earlier.

The missiles had returned to their bases.

The bombers had gone home.

Nobody had died.

For years, that had been presented as evidence that the security system worked.

Now a generation of Chinese students was asking a different question.

If the system had worked, why had they been forbidden to know how close it came to failing?

The next episode is about the government's answer.

And about what happened when that answer reached Peking University.

Thanks for listening.
