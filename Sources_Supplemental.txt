A Catastrophes supplemental.

No catastrophe this time.

Sources.

People have been asking what I used for the series.

The complete answer would require several thousand footnotes and would make very poor audio.

But I will publish it eventually.

So these are not the sources for the series.

They are the books I would give someone who wanted to start.

For the early Progressive period, Elaine Mercer’s The Progressive State, first published in nineteen ninety-eight, remains the best broad institutional history.

It is dry.

This is praise.

For the Fascist War, John Kennedy’s multi-volume history, published between nineteen eighty-two and nineteen eighty-seven, is still the standard place to begin.

If you can find the revised nineteen ninety-one edition of Volume Three, use that one.

Kennedy corrected several of the mobilization tables and removed an argument about French logistics that even he eventually admitted was indefensible.

Kennedy had strong opinions about Roosevelt, preparedness, and almost everything else.

You do not have to agree with him.

You should know what he argued.

For the Roosevelt problem more generally, Doris Kearns Goodwin and Daniel Reyes published Inherited in nineteen ninety-three.

The twenty-oh-eight annotated edition is the one I use.

Yes, the notes are worth it.

For the Atomic Settlement, I would start with the published Geneva records and the collected Morozov papers before reading any of the later grand theories.

If you want a single narrative account, Helena Varga’s Nobody Trusts Anybody, published in twenty thirty-one, is old now, but still unusually clear about what inspection and accounting were actually designed to accomplish.

There is also a photograph collection I should mention.

One of my colleagues at the University of Chicago, Mara Levin, published The Century in Pictures in twenty eighty-eight.

The title is inaccurate.

It covers nearly two centuries.

Historians are sometimes bad at titles.

Most of the images I have described in this series are reproduced there.

The Geneva Walk.

The empty weapon cases.

The red emergency tags at Harbin.

The Norderstedt circles.

The stalled tanks in Beijing.

Li Xinyi on the ladder.

If you want to see the pictures rather than listen to me describe them, that is the book.

For the World War, there is no single satisfactory book.

That is not false modesty.

There are simply too many wars inside the war.

But Amir Rao’s The War of Systems, published in twenty sixty-one, is probably the best place to begin if what interests you is how mobilization, orbital warfare, industrial exhaustion, and atomic reconstruction interacted.

For Seventy-Two, I recommend Liu Fen’s October Without Orders, published in twenty eighty-two.

Yes, the title is slightly misleading.

No, I do not think she should change it.

Lin Meiyu’s The Security Trap was published in twenty seventy.

Use the original Chinese edition if you read Chinese.

The first English translation softened several of her arguments about military institutions in ways Lin complained about publicly.

It is not primarily a history book.

Read it anyway.

It is much easier to understand the Second Shanghai Crisis after you have watched Lin take a bank run seriously for two hundred pages.

For Margaret Ward, use the papers.

Not the family biographies.

Certainly not the commemorative ones.

The Ward Presidential Library has digitized most of the major collections, including Thomas Ward’s Columbia files.

Use the twenty eighty-six digital critical edition rather than the older microfilm index.

Unless you enjoy being angry at catalog numbers.

Those boxes are less dramatic than the later biographies make them sound.

They are also more useful.

One last recommendation.

John Kennedy’s The Second Inspector.

Yes, the comic.

Six issues. Bad sales. Too many committees.

Kennedy thought he was writing about superheroes.

Historians have been arguing with him about that ever since.

Read it.

I still think it is worth your time.

Thanks for listening.
