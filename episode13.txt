@chunk 1

Book Nine, Episode One: It Was Raining.

It is June, twenty seventy-two.

Seven years have passed since the Second Shanghai Crisis.

Thirty years have passed since the World War ended.

More or less.

And over the last several episodes, the argument that began with Shanghai has become something larger.

Lin Meiyu was arrested.

The records continued to leak.

Students demonstrated.

Professors signed petitions.

Journalists published questions their editors had been instructed not to print.

Workers began attending university meetings.

Veterans began attending them too.

The government alternated between concessions, arrests, investigations, and statements explaining that the situation was under control.

It was not under control.

By June, demonstrations had spread well beyond Beijing.

There were marches in Shanghai, Nanjing, Guangzhou, Chengdu, Wuhan, and dozens of smaller cities.

Some demanded Lin's release.

Some demanded publication of the Shanghai records.

Some demanded independent newspapers.

Some demanded elections.

Some demanded that the government resign.

Many demanded several contradictory things at once.

This is normal.

Revolutions are tidier afterward.

The phrase SECURITY WITHOUT FEAR had become almost impossible to avoid.

It was painted on walls.

Printed on shirts.

Written across bedsheets hung from university windows.

People who disagreed completely about what should replace the existing system could usually agree that they wanted less fear in it.

Peking University remained one of the centers of the movement.

That was not historically inevitable.

During the World War, the university had been one of the great institutions of national mobilization.

Its engineers had worked on communications systems.

Its physicians had staffed emergency hospitals.

Its mathematicians had helped keep orbital and logistics networks operating while the country was being bombed.

For a generation afterward, that history had been taught as evidence that expertise and patriotism were the same thing.

By twenty seventy-two, students were asking whether patriotism sometimes required expertise to say no.

There is a photograph from June eighth that became famous later.

Students are standing in the rain outside the east gate.

A banner has sagged under the water until the middle almost touches the pavement.

Someone is holding an umbrella over a portable loudspeaker rather than over herself.

A young man in the foreground is laughing.

For years, captions identified him as one of the student organizers.

He was not.

He was waiting for his sister.

We know this because she complained about it in an interview thirty-four years later.

This is the point where I need to tell you something.

I was there.

Not in the photograph.

I checked.

I was nineteen years old, studying at Peking University, and I was somewhere behind the photographer, probably close enough to hear the loudspeaker.

I do not remember the young man.

I do not remember the banner.

I remember the rain.

And before we go any further, I need to be more careful than usual.

Because memory is evidence.

It is not particularly obedient evidence.

@chunk 2

I remember the rain beginning before lunch.

The meteorological record says it began at one-seventeen in the afternoon.

So that is already a problem.

I remember being wet before then.

Maybe I am remembering another day.

Maybe I had spilled something on myself.

Maybe the weather station was somewhere else in Beijing and the rain reached the campus first.

Or maybe nineteen-year-old me did not check the time.

This is what happens when historians become sources.

We become irritating.

The archival record is easier.

At twelve-forty-two, the university administration announced that the afternoon assemblies would not be permitted to use the main lecture court.

At one-oh-six, students began moving toward the east gate instead.

At one-thirty-three, campus security closed two vehicle entrances.

At one-forty-one, someone opened them again.

Nobody has ever established who.

At two-oh-five, the crowd was estimated at roughly nine thousand people.

By three, it was probably closer to fifteen thousand.

The speeches were not especially revolutionary.

That is important.

People remember the later slogans.

They remember the calls for elections.

They remember the demands that the Party surrender its monopoly.

Those came later.

On June eighth, much of the crowd was still asking for things the government could theoretically have granted.

Release Lin Meiyu.

Publish the Shanghai files.

End emergency censorship.

Allow independent investigation of the arrests.

Guarantee that students would not be punished for attending demonstrations.

There were more radical demands around the edges.

There always are.

But this was not yet a crowd trying to overthrow the state.

It was a crowd asking the state to explain itself.

Zhao Wen understood that distinction earlier than most of the government did.

By twenty seventy-two, Zhao was sixty-four years old and one of the most senior figures in the national security administration.

Her career had begun during the World War with rail priorities, power allocations, and emergency industrial planning.

She had spent the next three decades moving steadily upward through institutions built around one assumption:

security was the first responsibility of the state.

Zhao still believed that.

What had changed was her understanding of what counted as security.

In a closed meeting on June ninth, she warned that treating the demonstrations as an external threat would eventually make them one.

The surviving notes are incomplete.

One sentence appears in three separate accounts.

“The state exists to secure the people. The people do not exist to secure the state.”

Whether Zhao said it exactly that way is disputed.

She certainly expressed the idea.

By the end of the month, everyone would know the sentence.

At the time, most of the people in the room thought she was arguing for better crowd control.

She was not.

Outside, the rain continued.

I remember someone passing around paper cups of tea.

I remember a student standing on a concrete barrier trying to keep a cardboard sign dry under his shirt.

I remember being annoyed that nobody seemed to know what we were supposed to do next.

I do not remember feeling that history had begun.

People almost never do.

What I remember is being cold.

And then somebody said we were going to Tiananmen.

@chunk 3

We did not march to Tiananmen all at once.

That is another thing photographs tend to erase.

Groups left campus at different times.

Some took buses until the traffic stopped.

Some walked.

Some turned back.

Some joined halfway.

By early evening, the roads toward the center of Beijing were carrying a mixture of students, workers, office employees, veterans, curious families, and people who had simply decided they did not want to be somewhere else when something important might happen.

The government had not ordered the square closed.

Not yet.

Police were present in large numbers, but most units had been told to manage movement, not disperse it.

That distinction held longer than many people expected.

By seven-thirty, tens of thousands were in and around Tiananmen.

By nine, estimates range from eighty thousand to more than one hundred and twenty thousand.

The number matters less than the change in composition.

This was no longer a university demonstration.

Factory delegations arrived carrying work-unit banners.

Rail employees came in groups.

Hospital staff appeared still wearing badges from their shifts.

Veterans' organizations, which only a few years earlier had filled the same square demanding that China never again depend upon foreign restraint, began forming lines along the edge of the crowd.

Some of them wore World War ribbons.

Some carried photographs of Harbin.

The government noticed that.

Everyone did.

For decades, Tiananmen had been used to perform national unity.

During the World War, people had gathered there to mourn bombing raids and promise endurance.

Now many of the same symbols were being used to ask whether endurance had become obedience.

The official evening news showed almost none of this.

It reported demonstrations at several universities and said discussions with student representatives were continuing.

Technically true.

The broadcast spent more time on flood damage in Anhui.

Also technically true.

At ten-twelve, a student group projected the Shanghai crisis diagram onto the side of a building facing the square.

The image was enormous.

A rocket launch.

A failed satellite.

An American alert.

A Chinese alert.

Arrows feeding into more arrows.

Someone had added the words SECURITY WITHOUT FEAR underneath.

The crowd cheered.

I remember that.

There is video, so I am permitted some confidence.

What I remember differently is the silence afterward.

For years, I was certain the square went quiet for several seconds after the cheering stopped.

The recordings do not support this.

There is chanting almost immediately.

I have listened to them many times.

I can hear it.

I still remember silence.

That is not especially profound.

It is simply inconvenient.

Inside the Great Hall of the People, the senior leadership was meeting.

The disagreement was no longer about whether the demonstrations were serious.

It was about what kind of seriousness they represented.

One faction argued that the movement remained politically containable if the government released Lin, opened some of the Shanghai files, and allowed controlled investigations.

Another argued that every concession had produced a larger demand.

They were both correct.

Zhao Wen argued that this was not evidence against concessions.

It was evidence that the original problem had been larger than the government admitted.

That did not make her popular.

The meeting ended shortly after midnight without an order to clear the square.

That absence would matter.

At the time, it mostly meant everybody stayed wet.

@chunk 4

June became July.

The demonstrations did not end.

They changed.

The government released several detainees.

Lin Meiyu was not among them.

A partial collection of Shanghai documents was published.

The documents confirmed enough to make the withheld material more interesting.

Censorship rules were relaxed for university newspapers.

National newspapers discovered that the new rules did not apply to them.

Every concession solved one problem and exposed the next one.

This was not because the protesters had a secret plan to make compromise impossible.

Most did not have a plan at all.

It was because once people were allowed to ask one question in public, they discovered they had others.

Why had Shanghai been hidden?

Who authorized Lin's second detention?

Why could local officials be removed for corruption but national officials could not?

Why did the Party choose candidates for elections it then described as popular?

Why were military budgets classified from legislators who were expected to approve them?

Why were emergency powers still being renewed thirty years after the emergency that had created them?

That last question spread quickly.

It was not the loudest slogan.

It may have been the most dangerous.

The World War had created a Chinese state exceptionally good at mobilization.

It could move power.

Move food.

Move armies.

Move factories.

Move people.

It had become less practiced at explaining when movement was supposed to stop.

Zhao Wen had spent her career inside that machinery.

This made her dangerous to the people defending it.

On July sixteenth, she submitted a memorandum arguing that several emergency authorities should be suspended immediately and returned to ordinary statutory control.

The memorandum was not revolutionary.

It cited existing law.

It proposed committees.

It recommended deadlines.

This is sometimes how revolutions enter government buildings.

The hardliners rejected it.

Then someone leaked it.

By the following afternoon, students were carrying copies through Tiananmen.

One passage was underlined repeatedly:

“An emergency authority that cannot describe the conditions for its own termination is not temporary authority.”

Zhao had spent decades speaking like an administrator.

The streets had learned how to hear politics in it.

I remember reading the memorandum sitting on the pavement with three other students.

I remember thinking the language was boring.

This is embarrassing, given what I do for a living now.

One of the students beside me said, “She is saying they have to give the power back.”

I remember replying, “Then why doesn't she just say that?”

Apparently I was very sophisticated at nineteen.

By then, the demonstrations had spread into provincial capitals and industrial cities across the country.

Worker committees were beginning to coordinate with student groups.

Veterans were issuing statements asking the military not to be used for internal political disputes.

Several provincial Party organizations openly requested negotiations.

And for the first time, senior military officers began asking what would happen if they received an order to clear the square.

This is where Liu Jian returns to the story.

In twenty forty, Liu had been thirty-three years old, standing beside a field radio north of Qiqihar when Harbin disappeared from the communications map.

In twenty seventy-two, he was sixty-five and one of the most respected officers in the People's Liberation Army.

He had spent thirty-two years remembering what happened when governments decided that endurance itself was a strategy.

He had not yet been ordered to move against the demonstrations.

But people had begun asking him what he would do if he was.

He did not answer.

Not yet.

@chunk 5

By August, the demonstrations had become a national political crisis.

That phrase is useful because it sounds more organized than the reality was.

There was still no single opposition leadership.

No unified platform.

No agreed timetable.

Student committees argued with worker committees.

Worker committees argued with lawyers.

Lawyers argued with everyone.

Provincial reformers wanted negotiations.

Some student groups wanted immediate elections.

Others wanted only Lin's release and an end to emergency law.

A growing minority wanted the Party's monopoly ended entirely.

The government faced a movement that was becoming larger at the same moment it was becoming less coherent.

This made repression harder.

It also made negotiation harder.

Zhao Wen continued pressing for what she called constitutional normalization.

She still refused the language of revolution.

That was deliberate.

In public, she described the crisis as a failure of emergency government to return authority to ordinary institutions.

In private, she was becoming more direct.

A meeting record from August third quotes one hardline official asking whether she understood what would happen if the government admitted that the protesters had legitimate grievances.

Zhao answered:

“Yes.”

There is a pause in the transcript.

Then:

“We would have to address them.”

This was not considered helpful.

The hardliners had begun organizing around what would eventually become the National Emergency Committee.

They argued that foreign governments were exploiting the demonstrations.

There was evidence for some foreign involvement.

Money moved.

Foreign broadcasters encouraged the protests.

Intelligence services tried to establish contacts.

This was real.

It was also not an explanation for why millions of Chinese citizens were angry.

The distinction became increasingly difficult for the government to maintain.

On August nineteenth, the state television network broadcast a documentary about the World War.

The programming decision had been made weeks earlier.

The episode focused on the mobilization of Beijing in twenty forty.

Peking University laboratories.

Hospital volunteers.

Factory shifts.

Tiananmen filled with mourners after Harbin.

And, briefly, a young Zhao Wen standing beside an emergency power map.

The intention was obvious.

Remind the country what unity had accomplished.

The effect was not.

Students watched footage of people their own age being told that survival required sacrifice.

Veterans watched footage of themselves.

Families saw relatives who had never come home.

The next morning, someone painted a sentence across a wall near Peking University:

WE REMEMBER WHY YOU NEEDED THE POWER.

Underneath it, someone else added:

DO YOU?

I remember seeing that one.

I also remember thinking the second line was better.

There is no evidence I said so at the time.

By the end of August, even senior Party officials who opposed reform were beginning to doubt that the movement could simply be waited out.

The question changed.

Not whether the government would respond.

How.

And increasingly, that meant asking the military.

@chunk 6

The first formal request for military contingency planning was issued on September second.

Not an order to clear Tiananmen.

Not martial law.

Not yet.

The General Staff was asked to prepare options for protecting government buildings, communications centers, transport hubs, and strategic facilities if civilian authorities lost control.

This was ordinary contingency planning.

That phrase would become less comforting over the next several weeks.

Liu Jian received the request the same afternoon.

We have his copy.

It survives in the military archive with three handwritten marks.

The first circles the phrase “restore normal civil authority.”

The second underlines “minimum necessary force.”

The third is beside a paragraph authorizing commanders to secure strategic communications without further civilian approval if national command links failed.

Next to it, Liu wrote one word.

“Whose?”

That annotation became famous later.

At the time, nobody saw it except Liu and perhaps his staff.

He ordered the planning to proceed.

This is important.

Liu did not refuse.

He did not announce solidarity with the protesters.

He did not become a revolutionary general.

He did his job.

Units identified routes.

Fuel requirements were calculated.

Medical teams were assigned.

Communications plans were tested.

Commanders were told to prepare for crowd-control operations.

And throughout September, soldiers remained mostly outside central Beijing.

The protesters knew they were there.

Rumors multiplied.

Every truck became a troop movement.

Every troop movement became an impending assault.

I remember one night being told that armored units were fifteen minutes away from the square.

They were not.

The rumor arrived again forty minutes later.

Still not.

By the third time, people were making jokes about the world's slowest tanks.

That stopped being funny later.

Zhao Wen was now openly arguing against military intervention.

On September eighteenth, she addressed a closed meeting of senior Party and military officials.

We have the recording.

Her voice is tired.

She says that the state has spent thirty years teaching its officers that political authority must remain civilian.

Then she asks what exactly the government is asking the army to defend.

A building?

A committee?

A constitutional order?

A Party leadership?

Those things, she says, are not automatically identical.

The room becomes very quiet.

Unlike my memory of June eighth, the recording confirms this one.

For eleven seconds, nobody speaks.

The hardliners heard a threat.

Zhao insisted it was a question.

Liu Jian, seated three places away, said nothing.

That night, the demonstrations in Tiananmen were larger than they had been in weeks.

The rain had stopped months earlier.

I remember that because by then the square was dusty.

Blankets smelled like concrete.

People slept under banners instead of umbrellas.

And I remember realizing, for the first time, that I wanted to go home.

Not because I had changed my mind.

Because I was tired.

Because my mother had been calling every night.

Because history, when you are inside it, is mostly waiting around while being uncomfortable.

I did go home a few days later.

My cousin Sam came and got me.

A lot of others did not go home.

By October, the government would ask the army a question it had spent weeks trying not to ask directly.

And the army would have to answer.

That is where the next episode begins.

Thanks for listening.
