@chunk 1

This is Catastrophes. Book Ten, Episode Eight: The October Attempt.

It is October, twenty seventy-eight.

Six years have passed since Seventy-Two.

Five years since the Nanjing Settlement.

And in the United States, the argument is now about whether a republic can survive its own security state.

The answer, briefly, was unclear.

The crisis had been building for years.

And we have discussed this a few times.

The Treasury Crisis.

The failed proxy war.

The withdrawal from two Pacific basing agreements.

The collapse of another round of arms-reduction talks.

The emergency appropriations bills.

The classified continuity plans.

The increasingly public argument over whether the United States had become too weak to retreat and too exhausted to continue.

President Andrew Hale, who had taken office in twenty seventy-five, had spent most of his term trying to reduce military commitments without appearing to abandon allies.

This pleased almost nobody.

The security establishment thought he was moving too quickly.

The antiwar coalition thought he was moving too slowly.

Congress thought he was making too many decisions without Congress.

Hale thought Congress was making too few decisions at all.

By the autumn of twenty seventy-eight, the presidential election had become a referendum on the entire postwar American system.

The leading opposition candidate was Miriam Calder.

She was not expected to win.

By October, most polling had Hale ahead comfortably, and Calder's campaign was still being treated as an insurgent challenge rather than the probable next government.

That would change.

Quickly.

For now, what matters is that the election was five weeks away.

On October seventeenth, shortly after three in the morning, a group of senior national-security officials activated portions of the federal continuity system.

They did not announce a coup.

They announced an emergency.

Strategic communications centers were secured.

Several cabinet officials were temporarily isolated.

Military transport was redirected.

Access to parts of the federal communications network was restricted.

A small number of civilian officials were detained under authorities originally written for wartime continuity.

The organizers called themselves the National Continuity Group.

This name lasted less than a day.

History preferred the October Attempt.

Their stated purpose was limited.

Transfer temporary control of strategic forces to a restricted emergency command.

Suspend planned reductions.

Postpone the presidential election for ninety days.

Review foreign interference.

Restore what they called national decision capacity.

Then return authority to the ordinary constitutional government.

This was not how they described overthrowing the republic.

They did not believe they were overthrowing the republic.

That is important.

At three-forty-two, President Hale was moved to a secure federal site outside Washington.

Whether this was protective custody or detention became one of the first legal arguments of the morning.

Hale resolved it by asking whether he was free to leave.

He was told that departure was not recommended.

He asked again.

The answer did not improve.

At four-oh-eight, he dictated a statement.

“The election will occur on November fifth.”

The recording reached the public forty-three minutes later.

By then, Vice President Anna Roosevelt had already entered a continuity bunker beneath a federal complex in northern Virginia.

This was exactly what the emergency plan required her to do.

It was also the first thing she did that morning that made the situation worse.

@chunk 2

Anna Roosevelt was fifty years old.

She had been in national politics for almost two decades.

A senator from New York.

A committee chair.

A respected voice on security oversight.

The sort of politician described as serious by people who meant it as praise and boring by people who also meant it as praise.

The Roosevelt name still carried weight.

Less than it once had.

More than was probably healthy for anyone born with it.

Anna was not a great Roosevelt.

This judgment became fashionable later.

It is also unfair.

She was competent.

Careful.

Well prepared.

Good in hearings.

Good with budgets.

Good at asking questions that made witnesses wish they had prepared better answers.

In twenty sixty-five, during the investigations after the Second Shanghai Crisis, she had asked:

“If every office followed procedure, who was responsible for noticing that the procedures were interacting?”

It was a good question.

Thirteen years later, she was about to discover that being responsible for noticing a system was interacting was easier than being trapped inside one while it did.

At four-ten in the morning, Anna entered the continuity bunker.

At four-twelve, someone leaked that she had entered it.

The leak did not say why.

For the next forty-three minutes, a substantial portion of the country believed President Hale was dead, incapacitated, or had transferred power.

Financial markets opened early overseas.

The dollar fell sharply.

Two allied governments requested confirmation of who controlled American strategic forces.

Several military commands asked the same question.

Anna's staff issued a statement:

“The Vice President is safe and carrying out her constitutional responsibilities.”

This was intended to be reassuring.

It was not.

The phrase constitutional responsibilities immediately produced speculation that the Twenty-Fifth Amendment had been invoked.

It had not.

Anna authorized a second statement.

“The President remains President.”

This was clearer.

It also made people wonder why anyone had needed to say it.

At four-fifty-eight, Anna demanded direct communication with Hale.

The continuity system routed the request through the same restricted network being controlled by the National Continuity Group.

The Group approved the call.

In retrospect, this was probably a mistake.

At the time, it was not an irrational one.

They knew Anna Roosevelt well.

Careful.

Procedural.

Reluctant to improvise.

Deeply invested in continuity.

They expected her to calm Hale down.

They were mostly right about Anna.

They were wrong about Hale.

He had spent three years being accused of retreat, weakness, and indecision.

He had become very practiced at recognizing the difference between compromise and surrender.

The call did not make Anna less cautious.

It made Hale more certain.

That distinction would matter.

The recording of the call survives.

Hale begins by asking:

“Anna, are you part of this?”

There is a pause of just under two seconds.

Later commentators made a great deal of the pause.

They should not have.

Anna's answer is immediate enough for anyone who has ever been asked an offensive question before sunrise.

“No.”

Then:

“Are you?”

Hale laughed.

It may have been the last easy moment either of them had that day.

They agreed on three things.

The election would proceed.

Civilian control of strategic forces had to remain intact.

Neither of them would resign.

This sounds simple.

It was not.

Because outside the bunker, people were already beginning to act on the assumption that the government had split.

And once enough people act on an assumption, the distinction between assumption and event becomes difficult to maintain.

@chunk 3

By five-thirty in the morning, the October Attempt had become a problem of competing legitimacy.

The National Continuity Group controlled pieces of the communications system.

President Hale controlled the presidency.

Anna Roosevelt controlled almost nothing, but enough people thought she might control something that this became important.

Congress had not been dissolved.

The courts had not been closed.

Most military commands had not taken sides.

This was not a clean seizure of power.

It was an argument conducted through access lists, authentication codes, motor pools, secure telephones, and people asking who had authorized the person asking them to do something.

At five-forty-two, Anna ordered her staff to contact the Speaker of the House and the Chief Justice.

This was constitutionally sensible.

It was also leaked within minutes.

The resulting headline on one network read:

ROOSEVELT SEEKS SUCCESSION CONSULTATIONS.

She was not.

At six-oh-three, Anna issued another statement.

“No succession process is underway.”

This caused several commentators to ask why she was discussing succession at all.

Anna later described this period as “an education in the limits of clarification.”

That is generous.

At six-twenty, Hale managed to get a second recording out from the secure site where he was being held.

This one was shorter.

“The President of the United States has not transferred authority. No emergency body has constitutional power to postpone the election. Federal officers should obey lawful civilian command.”

The phrase lawful civilian command immediately became the center of the crisis.

The Group claimed it was acting under emergency continuity authority delegated by civilian officials.

Hale said those officials had no authority to delegate what they were delegating.

Anna agreed with Hale.

She then attempted to prove this by ordering a formal legal review through the executive emergency process.

This was exactly the process the Group was using to justify itself.

For roughly thirty minutes, both sides cited the same emergency framework against each other.

The White House legal office produced one interpretation.

The continuity lawyers produced another.

Congressional counsel produced a third.

By breakfast, constitutional law had become an operational weapon.

The military wanted none of this.

Several senior commanders issued statements affirming civilian control without identifying which civilians they meant.

One Pacific commander ordered all strategic forces in his area to maintain existing posture until the chain of command was clarified.

Another refused a request from the Group to redirect a communications aircraft.

A third complied with a logistics order and then publicly stated that compliance did not imply recognition of emergency authority.

This was not cowardice.

It was an attempt to avoid becoming the institution that decided who governed the country.

The memory of Seventy-Two was impossible to miss.

American officers had spent six years studying Liu Jian.

They had also spent six years insisting they would never need to make his decision.

By seven, some of them were beginning to understand that history rarely asks permission before repeating a question in another language.

Anna tried to help.

At seven-twelve, she called three regional military commanders directly.

This was a mistake.

Not because she asked them to support the Group.

She did not.

She asked them to follow the Constitution.

The problem was that a Vice President personally calling generals during a disputed succession crisis looked exactly like a Vice President personally building a command network.

One of the calls was recorded.

Another was summarized inaccurately.

A third was leaked before it ended.

For the next hour, rumors spread that Anna Roosevelt was preparing to assume emergency authority over the armed forces.

She was not.

At seven-fifty-eight, Hale sent her a message through an aide.

It contained six words.

“Anna. Please stop helping for ten minutes.”

She did.

This may have been one of the most effective decisions of the morning.

@chunk 4

The Attempt began to fail shortly after eight.

Not because the Group surrendered.

Because the number of institutions willing to treat its authority as temporary began to exceed the number willing to treat it as binding.

The first major break came from the Treasury Department.

At eight-oh-six, the Secretary of the Treasury ordered federal payment systems to continue recognizing only appropriations and signatures issued through the ordinary civilian chain.

This sounds less dramatic than troops in the streets.

It was not.

The Group could secure buildings.

It could restrict communications.

It could not easily run a government that could not move money.

At eight-twenty-one, the Speaker of the House announced that Congress would convene in emergency session at ten.

At eight-thirty-four, the Chief Justice issued a statement saying that no provision of the Constitution authorized postponement of a presidential election by executive emergency order.

At eight-forty-two, two federal appellate courts opened extraordinary proceedings.

At nine-oh-three, the first major military command publicly rejected the Group's authority to alter strategic-force control.

The statement did not mention Hale.

It did not mention Roosevelt.

It said only:

“Existing constitutional command relationships remain in force.”

That was enough.

Other commands followed.

The National Continuity Group had assumed that if it moved quickly enough, everyone else would prefer temporary order to dangerous uncertainty.

This had been a reasonable assumption in many earlier crises.

It was proving less reasonable in a country that had spent six years watching what happened when institutions asked who had the right to give an order.

By nine-thirty, the October Attempt was no longer expanding.

It was contracting.

That did not make it harmless.

Several officials remained detained.

The President was still not free to leave his secure site.

Parts of the federal communications system were still under contested control.

And nobody knew whether General Nathaniel Voss, the senior military officer most closely associated with the Group, intended to escalate.

Nathaniel Voss had been born in nineteen ninety-eight in Omaha, Nebraska.

He was eighty years old.

Too young to have served in the World War.

His father had fought in Europe.

His older sister had been killed in the Hamburg strike.

Voss had spent most of his career inside the postwar security establishment.

He was not a theatrical man.

No medals worn unnecessarily.

No speeches about destiny.

No apparent desire to become president.

His argument was simpler.

The United States had spent a decade retreating from commitments, losing allies, reducing strategic forces, and allowing domestic politics to interfere with what he considered basic national survival.

He believed the election could still happen.

Just not yet.

Ninety days.

That was the number.

Ninety days to review foreign interference.

Ninety days to stabilize strategic command.

Ninety days to reverse several planned withdrawals.

Then politics could resume.

This was the most dangerous thing about the October Attempt.

Its organizers had given themselves an ending.

They believed that made what they were doing temporary.

At ten-seventeen, Hale was finally given an unrestricted line to Congress.

He spoke for four minutes.

The sentence remembered afterward was the one he had already used before dawn:

“The election will occur on November fifth.”

But the line that mattered more that morning came later.

“A republic cannot preserve constitutional government by suspending constitutional government until conditions improve.”

Miriam Calder heard the speech from a campaign office in Philadelphia.

At the start of the day, she had been the opposition candidate most analysts expected to lose.

By lunch, that expectation was collapsing.

Her campaign cancelled its scheduled economic speech.

At eleven-forty, Calder appeared before reporters.

She did not attack Hale.

That surprised people.

Instead, she said:

“A republic cannot defend itself by suspending the thing it is defending.”

The line spread immediately.

Within hours, it was being quoted alongside Hale's.

For the first time in the campaign, the President and the woman trying to replace him were saying essentially the same thing.

The National Continuity Group had intended to postpone an election.

Instead, it had just given the election a subject.

@chunk 5

The October Attempt collapsed before sunset.

Not all at once.

At eleven-fifty-two, the National Continuity Group lost control of the federal communications hub it had been using to route emergency orders.

At twelve-oh-seven, the first detained cabinet official was released.

At twelve-thirty-one, General Nathaniel Voss ordered units under his direct authority to stand down from further emergency actions pending legal review.

At one-oh-four, Voss was informed that several regional commands had rejected further instructions from the Group.

At one-seventeen, Congress formally declared the emergency transfer of authority unconstitutional.

At one-forty-three, the Supreme Court issued an emergency order requiring the release of detained civilian officials.

At two-sixteen, the security detail at Hale's facility informed him that he was free to leave.

Hale asked whether this was now a recommendation.

Nobody laughed.

At three-oh-two, he returned to Washington.

Anna Roosevelt left the continuity bunker shortly afterward.

Her staff had prepared a statement.

She read it.

Then she refused to give it.

This was probably wise.

By four, the October Attempt was effectively over.

It had lasted less than thirteen hours.

This fact later became comforting.

It should not have.

Later accounts linger on Roosevelt because she is memorable.

A bunker.

A bad statement.

A worse clarification.

A President asking her to stop helping for ten minutes.

Those details are funny.

The event was not.

Senior officials used emergency authorities to detain civilians, seize communications, interfere with military command, and attempt to postpone a presidential election.

The United States had spent almost three centuries assuming that this was the sort of thing that happened somewhere else.

On October seventeenth, twenty seventy-eight, it happened there.

Miriam Calder said essentially the same thing that evening.

She stood outside her campaign headquarters in Philadelphia and spoke for less than five minutes.

Most of the speech has been forgotten.

One line has not.

“We should not congratulate ourselves because the republic survived a coup attempt.”

Then:

“We should ask why a coup attempt became possible.”

That question changed the election.

Until that morning, Calder had been the opposition candidate most analysts expected to lose.

By the next day, polling had moved sharply.

By the end of the week, the assumption that Hale would win reelection had disappeared.

Not because voters blamed him for resisting the Attempt.

They did not.

His approval rating rose.

That was the strange part.

The country admired what Hale had done.

It was becoming less certain that admiration was a reason to give him another term.

@chunk 6

Hale reached the same conclusion before most of his advisers did.

On October twenty-second, five days after the Attempt, he addressed the country from the White House.

He described the events of October seventeenth as an attempted unconstitutional transfer of authority.

He praised the military officers, civil servants, judges, legislators, and ordinary federal employees who had refused unlawful orders.

He defended Anna Roosevelt.

He did not defend himself.

Near the end of the speech, Hale said:

“I stopped it after it began.”

Then:

“I did not stop it from becoming possible.”

The line produced several seconds of silence in the room.

He continued.

“The men who tried to postpone the election are responsible for what they did. I am responsible for a government in which they were able to try.”

Hale then announced that he would not seek reelection.

This was not expected.

For approximately forty minutes, Anna Roosevelt became the presumptive nominee of the governing party.

This was the high point of her presidential prospects.

The party leadership met that evening.

Roosevelt had experience.

Name recognition.

A national organization.

The vice presidency.

Under ordinary circumstances, those things would have been enough.

These were not ordinary circumstances.

The Attempt had made continuity itself politically suspect.

And Anna had spent thirteen hours demonstrating, with unusual thoroughness, that procedural competence did not necessarily translate into crisis leadership.

She was not blamed for the coup.

She was not accused of disloyalty.

Nobody seriously suggested she had cooperated with the National Continuity Group.

They simply decided not to nominate her.

The party chose someone else.

This was, politically, the end of the Roosevelt dynasty.

There were still Roosevelts.

There are still Roosevelts now.

But after Anna, none would again hold national office of comparable importance.

The family that had spent more than a century appearing whenever American institutions needed rebuilding ended its political history with a Vice President who had tried very hard to preserve those institutions and discovered that the country wanted something else.

Anna accepted the decision publicly.

Privately, she was less gracious.

We know this because several people wrote it down.

I am inclined to forgive her.

Miriam Calder now faced Governor David Mercer, the replacement nominee, with five weeks to campaign.

She also faced a country that had spent most of the decade arguing about American decline and had suddenly discovered that the more immediate question was American government.

Calder did not campaign primarily against the men who had organized the Attempt.

They were being arrested.

She campaigned against the conditions that had made their argument plausible.

Permanent emergency authorities.

Unreviewed continuity powers.

Military commitments Congress could neither sustain nor end.

A security establishment whose responsibilities had expanded faster than its accountability.

And a political culture that had increasingly treated retreat from any commitment as evidence of national failure.

Her argument was not that the United States had become too weak.

It was that the country had confused strength with the inability to stop doing things.

On November fifth, twenty seventy-eight, the election occurred.

Hale voted.

Anna Roosevelt voted.

Nathaniel Voss voted from federal custody.

Miriam Calder won.

Not narrowly.

The next episode is about what she did with the victory.

And about how a country that had spent decades measuring its strength by how much of the world it could hold finally began deciding what it was willing to let go.

Thanks for listening.
